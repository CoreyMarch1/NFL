Methodology

Where the win probabilities come from

Every future matchup is priced with a 50/50 blend of two views: this week's composite power rating (the FPI/nfelo/Inpredictable/Unexpected Points/DVOA/PFF blend already built and validated for the weekly matchup model) and each team's 2026 season win total, taken from a September sportsbook consensus. The composite is precise but reflects a single week's form; the win total is a market's full-season read but doesn't update play by play. Blending them is a deliberate hedge against trusting either one alone this far in advance. The current week (Week 5) is the exception: it uses the fully-built weekly model directly — real market lines for all 15 games, the QB adjustment with confirmed Week 5 starters, and the injury point-adjustment — since that's strictly more information than the blend has for any other week. Chiefs and Panthers are on bye. The composite carries an Oct 7 refresh of its Inpredictable (betting-market) input.

A robustness check on this week's pick: the weekly model's win probabilities run 1–7 points below what the moneylines imply, so the plan was re-run with Week 5 priced straight off the no-vig moneylines instead. Same Week 5 pick — the Cowboys are also the market's biggest favorite among teams you haven't used (79.5%) — and not a single other week changed. Bucs–Cowboys is the Thursday game, so this week's pick locks in early.

Why this isn't just "pick the biggest favorite each week"

Survivor is a resource-allocation problem: using a great team in an easy week you didn't need them for is the same mistake as saving a mediocre team for a week you did. This roadmap treats the whole remaining season as one optimization — a weeks×teams assignment problem solved to maximize the product of every remaining week's win probability at once, not the sum of each week's best pick taken greedily. That's why a team can show up as a strong "alternative" in one week's detail panel while the plan actually uses them several weeks later: the math found a better overall path by saving them.

Pure survival vs. ownership-aware

Pure survival maximizes the probability of surviving every remaining week — the right objective if all that matters is lasting as long as possible. Ownership-aware also weighs how many other entries you'd outlast: a win on a lightly-picked team in a week where the popular pick might lose grows your share of the pool more than the same win alongside most of the field. For the current week, each team is valued at win probability × 1 / (share of the pool still alive if that team wins), using projected ownership from a model fit on this pool's own Weeks 1–4 picks. Later weeks stay pure survival: ownership can't be projected that far ahead, and keeping them on survival is what keeps the plan saving strong teams for the weeks that need them.

This week both settings pick the Cowboys. The Bengals have the best single-week value (lower win odds, but less than half the projected ownership), yet the full-season plan would rather keep them for Week 12. The call is fragile: if Cowboys ownership comes in about 10 points higher than projected — within the ownership model's typical error on popular picks — the ownership-aware pick flips to the Texans.

The load-bearing assumption: this plan is only as good as "the season plays out roughly as expected." A season is long — injuries, coaching changes, and bad weeks will move some of these probabilities before they matter. Full-season plans, revisited weekly, beat locking in and forgetting it.

What this can't see yet

  • Weekly injury reports, starter changes, and weather for any week past the current one — the same real-time signal the main weekly model uses for Week 5 doesn't exist yet for Week 6 onward.
  • Market line movement between now and kickoff, which often carries sharp information (injuries, weather) before it's public.
  • Opponent quality drift — a team's composite rating is this week's snapshot, held static for every future week rather than re-derived as the season actually unfolds.
